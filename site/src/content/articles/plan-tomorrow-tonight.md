Waking up without a plan is one of the easiest ways to lose a day. You check your phone "to see what's going on", answer a few messages, maybe watch a video, and by the time you've decided what to work on, half the morning is gone.

The fix takes about five minutes, and you do it the night before.

## The 3-to-5 card

Every evening, before bed:

1. Look at everything on your plate: assignments, revision, chores, deadlines.
2. Choose **three to five** things that genuinely matter for tomorrow. Not ten. Three to five.
3. Write them on a small card or sticky note and leave it on your desk.

The next day, work through the card *before* you let anything else (messages, email, "quick" admin) take over. When the card is done, the day is a success, and anything extra is a bonus.

This idea is often credited to entrepreneur Marc Andreessen, who wrote about using index cards to stay focused. It works because it forces you to **prioritise when you're calm**, instead of deciding in the moment when every notification seems urgent.

In the [workspace](/workspace), you can do the same thing: add tomorrow's three to five tasks, mark the most important as *High* priority, and you'll see them waiting first thing.

## Watch out for "motion"

There's a sneaky trap in planning: it *feels* productive. Researching the best study method, reorganising your notes, making a beautiful revision timetable… all of it gives you a little hit of satisfaction, but none of it actually moves you forward on its own.

It helps to separate:

- **Motion**: planning, researching, organising, preparing. Necessary in small doses.
- **Action**: the actual work that produces a result. Writing the paragraph, solving the problem, testing yourself on the flashcards.

If you've spent more time this week planning to study than studying, that's your sign. Cap planning at a few minutes, then take action.

## Set goals you control

"Get an A in maths" is an *outcome* goal. You want it, but you can't control it directly, and if you don't hit it, it can feel like all your effort was wasted.

Switch to **input goals** that are completely in your control:

- "Do 20 practice questions every weekday for the next four weeks."
- "Complete two focus sessions on my dissertation every morning."
- "Review my flashcards for 15 minutes after dinner."

Input goals are measurable, achievable every single day, and they're exactly what tends to produce the outcome anyway.

## Use deadlines to your advantage

**Parkinson's law** says that work expands to fill the time available for it. Give yourself a week to write an essay and it'll take a week. Give yourself two days and, somehow, it'll take two days.

You can use this deliberately:

- Set your own deadlines *before* the official one.
- Give each focus session a specific mini-goal: "By the end of this pomodoro I'll have the introduction drafted."
- Plan in short **sprints** rather than vague long timelines. A 12-week semester plan with weekly targets feels far more urgent (and doable) than "this year I'll get better at Spanish".

When every week has a clear target, every day has a clear purpose.

## Bounce back fast

Some days will go wrong. You'll oversleep, feel ill, or get pulled into something unexpected. That's life.

Instead of measuring yourself by unbroken streaks, which shatter the moment you miss a day, measure your **recovery speed**: how quickly do you get back on track after a slip? An hour? A day? A week?

Getting faster at restarting is one of the most valuable skills a student can build. If today fell apart, don't write off the whole week. Write a new card tonight, keep it small, and start again tomorrow.

## Focus on one main thing

If you're juggling lots of goals at once (top grades, a side project, learning guitar, getting fit), you can end up making a little progress on everything and real progress on nothing.

Try the **80/20 approach**: for the next few months, give about 80% of your focused energy to *one* primary goal, and fit the others into smaller, scheduled slots. You can always rotate priorities later.

## Your five-minute evening routine

1. Open your task list and clear out anything already done.
2. Pick tomorrow's 3–5 most important tasks.
3. Break the first one into a tiny starting step.
4. Lay out what you'll need on your desk.
5. Close the laptop, knowing exactly how tomorrow begins.

That's it. Plan tonight, act tomorrow, and let the [timer](/workspace) do the rest.

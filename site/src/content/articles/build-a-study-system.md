Every student in your class wants a good grade. Every applicant wants the job. Goals are important for giving you direction, but they rarely explain why one person succeeds and another doesn't, because **everyone has roughly the same goals.**

What's different is the *system*: the small, repeatable things you do every day. As James Clear puts it in *Atomic Habits*, you don't rise to the level of your goals; you fall to the level of your systems.

## Focus on inputs, not outputs

Your grade at the end of term is a *lagging* result: it's the delayed outcome of hundreds of earlier choices. You can't change it directly today.

What you *can* control today are the inputs:

- Did I do my two focus sessions?
- Did I review my flashcards?
- Did I start the reading before the lecture?

Get the inputs right consistently, and the outputs tend to take care of themselves. This is why our [workspace](/workspace) tracks sessions and focus minutes rather than grades. They're the part you control.

## The surprising maths of small improvements

Small daily actions feel pointless in the moment. Reading for ten minutes won't change your life today. But habits compound like interest.

If you got just 1% better at something every day for a year, you'd end up roughly **37 times better** (1.01³⁶⁵ ≈ 37.8). If you got 1% worse every day, you'd decline to almost nothing (0.99³⁶⁵ ≈ 0.03). Real life isn't that tidy, but the principle holds: tiny, consistent effort beats occasional heroic effort.

## Design your environment

We like to think we're driven by willpower, but most of the time we just do whatever is easiest. So make the good things easy and the distracting things hard.

**Add friction to distractions:**

- Keep your phone in another room while studying.
- Log out of social apps on your laptop, or use a website blocker during study hours.
- Turn your phone's screen to greyscale. It's amazing how much less tempting it becomes.

**Remove friction from good habits:**

- Lay out your books and notes the night before.
- Keep a water bottle and snacks at your desk so you don't wander off.
- Bookmark your study playlist or the [workspace](/workspace) so starting is one click.

## Use "100%" rules

Moderation sounds sensible ("I'll only check my phone a little bit while studying"), but it forces you to make the same decision over and over. Every decision costs energy, and eventually you give in.

Strangely, an absolute rule is often *easier*: "No phone during focus sessions, ever." There's nothing to negotiate, so there's nothing to resist. Pick one or two 100% rules that matter most to you and stick to them.

## Stack new habits onto old ones

New habits stick better when they're attached to something you already do. The formula is simple:

> **After I [current habit], I will [new habit].**

For example:

- After I make my morning tea, I'll write down my three priorities for the day.
- After I sit down at my desk, I'll start a 25-minute pomodoro before doing anything else.
- After dinner, I'll review my flashcards for ten minutes.

The existing habit becomes a reliable trigger, so you don't have to remember or rely on motivation.

## Never miss twice

You will miss days. Everyone does. You'll get sick, have a busy weekend, or just have a rubbish day. Missing once is not the problem.

The danger is missing *twice in a row*, because that's when a slip quietly becomes a new pattern. So adopt one rule: **never miss twice.** If you skipped yesterday, today's only job is to do *something*, even a single short session. Getting back on track quickly matters far more than keeping a perfect streak.

## Get it out of your head

Finally, a good system needs a place to put things. Your brain is great at having ideas but terrible at storing them. Trying to remember every deadline and to-do creates a constant background hum of stress.

Write everything down in one trusted place (a notes app, a notebook, or the workspace's tasks and notes) and review it regularly. Once your brain trusts that nothing will be forgotten, it relaxes, and you can actually focus on the one thing in front of you.

## Start small

Don't try to adopt everything here at once. Pick **one** environment change and **one** habit stack, and run them for two weeks. Systems are built a brick at a time, a bit like a village.

There are two kinds of studying. There's the kind where you sit at your desk for six hours, half-reading notes while replying to group chats, checking email and glancing at videos. And there's the kind where you sit down for ninety minutes, do nothing but the work, and walk away having actually learned something.

The second kind is what author Cal Newport calls **deep work**: focused, uninterrupted effort on something mentally demanding. It's rare, it's valuable, and it's a skill you can train.

## Deep work vs shallow work

**Shallow work** is anything you can do while distracted: tidying files, answering messages, rewriting notes neatly, watching lectures at 2× speed while scrolling. It feels busy, but it rarely moves you forward much.

**Deep work** is the hard stuff: practice problems, essay writing, actively testing yourself, working through a proof you don't understand yet. It's uncomfortable precisely because it stretches you, and that's why it works.

A useful rule of thumb: **one or two hours of genuine deep work often produces more than a full day of scattered effort.**

## Why switching tasks is so costly

Every time you switch from your work to a notification and back, a little of your attention stays behind on the thing you just looked at. This "attention residue" means you return to your work slower and less sharp, even if the interruption only took a few seconds.

Do that thirty times an afternoon and you never get fully into the work at all. The fix isn't more willpower; it's fewer switches.

## Separate "maker" time from "manager" time

Programmer and essayist Paul Graham described two kinds of schedules:

- A **manager's schedule** is chopped into small blocks: meetings, messages, quick decisions. It's fine for admin.
- A **maker's schedule** needs long, open blocks of time to build, write or solve problems. A single interruption in the middle can wreck an entire afternoon.

Students need both, but they mix them constantly. The trick is to **keep them separate.** For example:

- **Morning (maker):** two or three focus sessions on your hardest subject, with phone and messages off.
- **Afternoon (manager):** emails, group-project chat, organising notes, booking things, lighter reading.

Many people find their mind is sharpest earlier in the day, so protecting the first few hours for deep work is a popular approach. But if you're a night owl, guard your evening instead. The important thing is that the block is protected.

## Create quiet-time rules

Pick specific times that are always reserved for deep work, such as "weekday mornings until 11" or "Tuesday and Thursday afternoons". During those blocks:

- Phone in another room, or at least on do-not-disturb and out of sight.
- Messaging apps and email closed.
- Tell friends or family you're unavailable, and when you'll be back.
- One task at a time, chosen *before* the block starts.

Making it a fixed rule rather than a daily decision is what makes it stick.

## Set up a deep-work space

Your brain learns associations. If your desk is where you study, scroll, game and eat, it doesn't know what it's there for. Try to make one spot (even just one side of a table) your focus spot. Then add a consistent starting ritual:

1. Clear the desk except for what you need.
2. Fill your water bottle.
3. Put on the same [background sound](/articles/choosing-study-sounds) you always use for focus.
4. Open the [workspace](/workspace), pick your task and press start.

After a few weeks, the ritual itself starts to put you in the mood to focus.

## Build your focus stamina

If deep work feels exhausting at first, that's normal. Focus is like a muscle that's been under-used. Start with what you can manage, even a single 25-minute session, and gradually build up: two sessions, then three, then a 50-minute block.

Track it. Seeing your daily focus minutes climb (the workspace's Village tab shows the last seven days) is one of the most motivating things there is.

## The takeaway

You don't need more hours. You need **better** hours. Protect a block of time, remove the distractions, do one hard thing, and stop. Then enjoy your evening without guilt, because you actually got the important work done.
